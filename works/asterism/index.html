Your sky

    About the figures

    What you draw here is an asterism — an unofficial figure. Astronomers recognize 88 official constellations; the International Astronomical Union fixed their names and boundaries between 1922 and 1930, and since then every star in the sky belongs to exactly one. Every other figure anyone has ever traced in the stars is an asterism. The Big Dipper is one: seven bright stars that are only part of the official constellation Ursa Major. So are yours.

    The same stars have never meant one thing. The Dipper's seven are a Plough in Britain, a Wagon in Germany, the Northern Dipper — Beidou — in China, the Seven Sages in India, and to the ancient Egyptians a bull's foreleg. The Pleiades cluster is the Seven Sisters of Greek myth, Subaru — "gathered together" — in Japan, and Matariki to Māori; its reappearance marks the Māori New Year. Parallel figures, not the same figure: the stars are shared; the seeing never was.

    And some figures are drawn in the dark itself. Many Aboriginal Australian nations — each with its own name and its own story — trace a great Emu not in stars but in the dark dust lanes of the Milky Way: Gawarrgay to the Kamilaroi and Euahlayi peoples, Gugurmin to the Wiradjuri, Tchingal to the Boorong people of north-west Victoria. Its head is the dark Coalsack nebula beside the Southern Cross; its neck and body follow the dust toward Scorpius and Sagittarius. In Kamilaroi and Euahlayi traditions, when the Emu stands full-length in the autumn evening sky, legs outstretched, it signals that emu eggs are ready to collect (Fuller, Norris, Trudgett and Anderson, 2014). A figure made of darkness is not an asterism — the term is "dark constellation" — and it is not this page's to redraw: these traditions are living knowledge held by their communities, and what is told here is only what those communities have published with researchers. This sky's dark lanes are a tip of the hat, nothing more.

    Your figures stay in your browser — this page keeps no record of them anywhere else. Two people at this same sky will see the same two hundred stars and none of each other's lines, which is roughly how it has always worked.
