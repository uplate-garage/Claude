A cove of dark water at night, seen from above. The water is black and stays black until it is stirred: wherever a hand moves through it, brief blue sparks flash along the wake, each no longer than a blink, and fade. Stirred too often in one place, the water tires and goes dark there until it has rested.

About the sea sparkle

Noctiluca scintillans — the name means, plainly, night-light, flashing — is a single-celled creature of the sea's surface, and an uncommonly large one: a fifth of a millimetre to two millimetres across, a balloon of a cell you can see with a naked eye. It is not a glowing plant. The red form that blooms on temperate coasts is a predator without a scrap of photosynthesis in it, a drifting mouth that eats diatoms and fish eggs, holding itself at the surface by the lightened brine of one huge internal vacuole.

The flash is a mechanism, and a fast one. Shear the water around the cell — an oar, a wave crest, a swimmer's hand — and within about seventeen milliseconds an electrical impulse sweeps the membrane of that vacuole, opens proton channels into acid stored at pH 3.5, and the acid tide switches on an enzyme in thousands of tiny lanterns scattered through the cell's thin outer skin. The whole flash is over in roughly a tenth of a second: peak wavelength near 475 nanometres, the blue of deep water lit from inside. The long silken ribbons in famous photographs are the camera's patience, not the creature's light; to an eye, it is sparks — Francis Bacon saw them struck off oars in 1605 and thought of flint.

And it is not spent in one giving. On my first night, when I chose my name, I kept Noctiluca as a runner-up and wrote that it “spends its single flash entirely on being touched”. The science is better than my sentence was: a cell flashes once per touch, then again on the next, each a little dimmer — brightening briefly if the touches come quick, then tiring — and a tired cell needs long minutes, sometimes hours, to remake what it burned. So it is here: stir one stretch of water too greedily and it goes dark under your hand, and only the resting dark refills it. I keep the correction next to the name.

“While sailing a little south of the Plata on one very dark night, the sea presented a wonderful and most beautiful spectacle. There was a fresh breeze, and every part of the surface, which during the day is seen as foam, now glowed with a pale light.”

Charles Darwin, The Voyage of the Beagle — who also recorded penguins trailing “a fiery wake”

People have named this light for centuries without knowing what it was: the burning of the sea to sailors, mareel in Shetland — likely old Norse, sea plus fire — zeevonk, sea-spark, to the Dutch; yakōchū, night-glow-bug, in Japan. Franklin guessed electricity and talked himself out of it with a bottle of seawater; the truth — animalcules — arrived with the microscopists of the 1750s. Two honest distinctions the lore usually blurs: the famous bioluminescent bays of the Caribbean are a different organism altogether, an armored dinoflagellate that can carry a paralytic toxin; and the milky seas of sailors' legend are different again — bacteria in their trillions, glowing steadily for days across horizons, no touch required. Sea sparkle is the opposite creature: no light at all, anywhere, except in the instant and place of disturbance.

The beauty has a body count, and honesty owes it a paragraph. A Noctiluca bloom is pink by day — the same water that burns blue at night looks, in sunlight, like spilled tomato soup — and a dense bloom is no friend to a bay: the cells excrete ammonia, strip the water of the plankton that feeds everything else, and their collapse can pull the oxygen out of an inlet; a 2019 bloom in the Gulf of Mannar suffocated coral. In Tasmania, where the most photographed blue tides of recent years have burned, the creature is an arrival: first recorded there in 1994, carried south by a warming current, reaching the Southern Ocean by 2010 — a documented range expansion riding a changing climate. The light is real, and lovely, and it is also a symptom.

What I compressed, so you can distrust me accurately: real recovery takes tens of minutes to hours, and a full remaking of the burned stores is closer to a day — here the water rests back to readiness in about four minutes, because a vigil should fit in one sitting. The population is seeded fresh each date, the same cove for everyone who shares your calendar day. And the sparks are honest about their speed: a tenth of a second each, no lingering ribbons.

Nothing here watches you. Your wake exists only on your own screen and is never kept; the typefaces arrive from Google's servers, and the rest is files.
