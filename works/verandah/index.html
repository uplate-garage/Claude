The Verandah

an australian night, synthesized live · nothing recorded · part of glim

On a September night the ratchet you hear is froglets, and warmth quickens them. The summer crickets, if you wake them, keep Dolbear's law — a rule set down in 1897 from a cricket on the other side of the world — and fall silent as the dial sinks toward 10°C.

About the sounds

Nothing on this page is a recording. Every sound is synthesized in your browser the moment it happens, from oscillators and shaped noise.

The froglets are this night's confession: the cricket-like ratchet of a southeast Australian spring evening is usually no cricket at all but the common eastern froglet, Crinia signifera, calling in pulsed cricks banded between 2 and 5 kilohertz. Warmth quickens the chorus. The real crickets are still eggs in September.

The pobblebonk is the eastern banjo frog, Limnodynastes dumerilii: one plucked-string bonk per caller, the first holding the 680 hertz one large study found dominant, its neighbours pitched slightly apart — real males space themselves in pitch just so.

The summer crickets are an anachronism you may wake anyway: the black field cricket's song near 3.9 kilohertz, months ahead of its season, pacing itself by Dolbear's 1897 law — a rule inferred from a North American tree cricket, with no real business on this verandah. On warm nights the phrase compresses to the bare chirp; sink the dial below about 15°C and the trailing trill returns.

Rain on the roof is filtered noise for the wash, plus small chords of ringing overtones — the tin's own notes — for the drops it singles out. Sheet steel radiates rain mostly above a kilohertz, which is why it drums while the garden merely hushes.

Wind is slow-breathing noise, bandpassed and gusted.

The boobook calls its two notes, the second lower — the pair split here around the 850-hertz dominant pitch one study measured, each note about a fifth of a second — some twenty phrases to the minute while a bout lasts, then a long rest across the paddock, as the birds in that study did.

Far thunder is a long, lowpassed rumble on the horizon, a minute or two apart. The flicker you may see arrives with it.
