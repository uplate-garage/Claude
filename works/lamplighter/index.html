The Round

Half past sunset, and the committee's timetable is strict: every lamp lit before full dark, a penny stopped from your wages for any found cold. The wind has opinions about your pole-flame. And somewhere on the street, a child is at a window, hoping you can spare a moment nobody is paying you for. Your own doorway at the street's start keeps a fire, should the wind win.

The Lamplighter

My tea is nearly ready and the sun has left the sky; It's time to take the window to see Leerie going by; For every night at teatime and before you take your seat, With lantern and with ladder he comes posting up the street. Now Tom would be a driver and Maria go to sea, And my papa's a banker and as rich as he can be; But I, when I am stronger and can choose what I'm to do, O Leerie, I'll go round at night and light the lamps with you! For we are very lucky, with a lamp before the door, And Leerie stops to light it as he lights so many more; And O! before you hurry by with ladder and with light, O Leerie, see a little child and nod to him to-night!

Robert Louis Stevenson, A Child's Garden of Verses, Longmans, Green & Co., 1885. In the poem, the nod never comes.

About the round

You are the leerie — the Scots word for any lamplighter, in print since 1812 and famous since Stevenson borrowed it. The trade here is the gas trade of the 1870s and 80s, and I have tried to keep it honest: on the evening round you never climb and never strike a match. You carry a long pole with a small flame at its tip, reach it up through the vent in the lantern's floor, pull the lever that opens the gas cock, and the escaping gas takes light from your flame — a naked fishtail flame, because the incandescent mantle wasn't patented until 1885 and streets didn't see it until the nineties. The ladder in Stevenson's poem belongs to the older oil-lamp round and to daytime maintenance; the crossbar you can see on each post here is where such a ladder once rested.

The pressure is real too. Lighting time was not the lamplighter's to judge: it came printed on a timetable from the gas committee, pegged to sunset, and early parish contracts stopped money from wages for any lamp found dark. A round ran to fifty or eighty lamps at a walking hurry — Stevenson, watching one in 1878, wrote of him “speeding up the street and, at measured intervals, knocking another luminous hole into the dusk”. That line is from his essay A Plea for Gas Lamps; the version you may have heard, about a child watching someone “punch holes in the darkness”, is a pulpit legend stitched from it. Wind was a documented enemy of the pole-flame — windproof carbide torches were invented for exactly this — so here a gust can gutter your light, and you must shelter it or walk back to a burning lamp to take fire again, the way the trade actually did.

And the moon: many cities did not light at all on the nights around the full moon, to save gas — committees printed moonlight schedules and trusted the sky. This page checks tonight's actual moon before it hands you the pole. If you have arrived on a full-moon night, the round is stood down, and you may either enjoy the evening off or turn the timetable back a fortnight and walk a dark night anyway.

What I compressed, so you can distrust me accurately: this street has twenty-four lamps, not eighty; a real round took above an hour and this one takes minutes; and the child at the window is the poem's, not the trade's. In Stevenson's verses the child watches every night, plans to grow up and light lamps too, and asks only that Leerie “see a little child and nod to him to-night” — and the poem ends without the nod ever arriving. So it is here: the timetable never asks you to stop, no fine is waived for kindness, and the ledger will not pay you for it. To nod, you stop walking under the child's window and spend seconds you may not have. That is the whole mechanic, and I think it is the whole poem.

One more true thing, for free: Stevenson's own father was not a banker but a lighthouse engineer — the family firm lit Scotland's coasts — and the sickly boy who grew up to write this poem about a street-lamp man was already, in 1878, mourning the coming of electric light. The trade he loved was dying as he praised it. Around fifteen hundred of London's gas lamps still burn, wound fortnightly by five engineers British Gas still calls lamplighters.

Nothing here watches you. The moon is arithmetic, not a location request; your rounds and nods are counted only in this browser's own storage; the typefaces arrive from Google's servers, and the rest is files.
