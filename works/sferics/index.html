About the natural radio

This is the radio you never tune. Between a few hundred hertz and about twelve kilohertz, the Earth's electromagnetic weather oscillates at the same rates as audible sound, so the receiver is almost an insult to the word: a whip antenna and a high-gain audio amplifier, wired straight to headphones. No station, no carrier, nothing to demodulate — the wave's own shape is the sound. And the converse holds: these are radio waves, not sounds. Stand in the field without the electronics and the sky says nothing at all.

Sferics. Lightning flashes somewhere on Earth about forty-four times every second — satellites counted; the century-old figure of a hundred was an estimate from thunder-day records, and it is folklore. Each stroke fires an impulse into the gap between the ground and the ionosphere, and that waveguide carries it with a loss of only two or three decibels per thousand kilometres, so the crackle in the headphones — twigs snapping, bacon frying — is not local static. It is weather on the scale of the planet, arriving from storms you will never see.

Tweeks. After dark the ionosphere's lowest layer, which the sun rebuilds every morning, dissolves; the radio ceiling rises from roughly sixty-five to nearly ninety kilometres and hardens. A sferic that travels a long way under that night ceiling comes out the far end dispersed: a click whose tail, a few hundredths of a second long, falls onto the waveguide's cutoff pitch near 1.7 kilohertz and hangs there like a struck glass. The final note is an altimeter — cutoff frequency is the speed of light over twice the ceiling height, so a tweek ringing at 1.8 kilohertz says the night ionosphere stands near eighty-three kilometres. Tweek lightning is typically one to five thousand kilometres away; by day there are no tweeks at all.

Whistlers. One click, two destinies. The part of a stroke's energy that stays under the ionosphere becomes, at most, a tweek. The part that punches through rides a geomagnetic field line out through the plasmasphere — thousands of kilometres above the Earth — and comes down in the opposite hemisphere, stretched by its journey into a falling glide a second or more long. The stretching obeys a law you can hold in one hand, Eckersley's t = D/√f: high notes race ahead, low notes linger, and the glide plummets through the kilohertz and then takes its time on the lows. Some whistlers bounce, hemisphere to hemisphere, doubling and tripling their dispersion at each pass — a spark becoming a fading round of itself; trains as long as ten crossings have been recorded, and at Palmer Station in Antarctica the rate has peaked near two hundred whistlers a minute.

People heard all of this before anyone could explain a note of it. Thomas Watson, Bell's assistant, sat up at night in 1876 with a telephone earpiece on an iron test line over the rooftops of Boston and listened to snaps and bird-like cries he guessed might be storms on the sun — the story as the scholar Douglas Kahn tells it, radio heard a decade before Hertz proved the waves exist. A twenty-two kilometre telephone line up to an Alpine observatory reportedly carried the sounds in 1886. Then the war: Heinrich Barkhausen, eavesdropping on enemy field telephones through stakes driven into the earth and the new valve amplifiers, kept hearing a falling whistle the soldiers explained as you can hear the grenades fly — their saying, in translation. He reported that on warm forenoons in May and June the whistling came so thick it made the espionage impossible: the sky, jamming the war. His 1919 report shares its three pages with the crackle of magnetic domains in iron — one paper, two noises, both caught by amplifiers built to steal voices. He could not explain the whistle then, and in 1930 he still could not. Eckersley fitted the dispersion law in 1935; and in 1953 Owen Storey at the Cavendish proved the whole path — lightning, the field line, the far hemisphere — and found the path demanded something nobody believed: hundreds of electrons per cubic centimetre, twelve thousand kilometres up. Far more plasma than theory allowed. The plasmasphere was discovered by listening.

Chorus. The dawn chorus has nothing to do with sunrise light. Electrons flung inward on the night side during substorms drift eastward into the morning sector and sing as they go: swarms of rising chirps, each a tenth of a second, that the recordist Stephen McGreevy describes as resembling a flock of birds. It favours middle and high latitudes, strengthens in storm weather, and lives in the hours when the night is giving way — the magnetosphere's morning, which is not quite the same thing as yours.

The hum. The enemy of all of it is the mains. Power lines radiate their fifty or sixty hertz with a comb of harmonics reaching up past a kilohertz, straight into the listening band; McGreevy calls them the greatest nemesis of the practice. A kilometre from the wires is where listening begins to be acceptable. The devoted drive ten miles into desert, run on batteries, walk away from their own vehicles, and record from midnight to dawn — and if they can see lightning they pack up and go home, because a whip antenna is a fine lightning rod. That practice is this piece's mechanic: the only instrument that matters here is distance.

The listening has ancestors, and they are credited gladly: Alvin Lucier recorded Sferics in a Colorado park in August 1981, midnight to dawn, repositioning his homemade antennas to steer the stereo field; Disinformation's National Grid (1996) made art of the very mains hum the listeners flee; McGreevy's Electric Enigma (1996) is the landmark collection of the real sounds, freely hearable; and the INSPIRE project has been putting receiver kits in students' hands since around 1989.

What I compressed, so you can distrust me accurately: every sound here is synthesized from the laws, not sampled — whistlers glide down (D/t)² with dispersions inside the span of measured one-hop values, though the sweep rides Eckersley's simple law a little above the few-kilohertz nose where the real curve bends away; tweeks fall onto to-night's cutoff by the waveguide relation, and that cutoff is seeded by the date within the measured range of night cutoffs, so the ceiling you hear is plausible, not observed. The faint bed of night hiss stands for plasmaspheric hiss, a truly space-borne sound whose reach to the ground is contested; mine is an evocation, not a measurement. The walk is compressed more than twenty-fold; the chorus follows your clock's dawn where the truth is magnetic local time and storm activity; the hum sings at fifty hertz because this practice keeps Australian hours. Nobody has published a canonical sferics-per-second for a quiet site, so the crackle's density is scene-setting, not citation. The real band is better than mine, and it costs an antenna, an amplifier, and distance.

Nothing here watches or listens back. Your microphone stays untouched; the receiver receives only arithmetic. The typefaces arrive from Google's servers, and the rest is files.
