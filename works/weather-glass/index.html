About the glass

A storm glass is a sealed vial of camphor, two salts, alcohol and water, mixed so the camphor sits close to the edge of staying dissolved. Cool it and crystals come out of solution — feathery bottom growth like fern leaves, a fine snow that forms in the liquid and settles, now and then a floating star. Warm it and they melt away, more slowly than they grew. For a century and a half, printed tables have promised that these forms foretell the weather.

They do not. The glass was refuted in the very year of its greatest fame: while Vice-Admiral Robert FitzRoy's Weather Book (1863) was defending it, Charles Tomlinson published a systematic examination in the Philosophical Magazine concluding the storm glass “acts as a crude kind of thermoscope, inferior, for most of the purposes of observation, to the thermometer”. Modern crystallography closed the loop: the amount of crystal follows the temperature down the camphor solubility curve (Williams 2025), the pattern's character is set by how fast the cooling comes (Tanaka, Hagano, Kuno & Nagashima 2008), crystals grow quickly on cooling and dissolve at their leisure on warming (Mitsuya, Takahashi & Nagashima 2014) — and no sealed vial can feel the barometer at all. When it seems to work, it is because a fast cold snap often rides in with a front. A coin does nearly as well.

FitzRoy did not invent it — storm glasses were made a century before him, inventor unknown — and his own claim for it was not pressure but something stranger: that the mixture “varies in character with the direction of the wind — not its force”, growing “like fir, yew, fern leaves, or hoar frost” as the wind veers polar, by way of “electrical tension”. He had his glasses made by Negretti & Zambra and lent his name, willingly and then commercially, to a device that only ever read the room.

Hold that against what the same man truly built. Captain of the Beagle at twenty-three on her second survey voyage, the one who chose Darwin for a companion. Head, from 1854, of the little office that became the Met Office. After the Royal Charter Storm of October 1859 drowned some eight hundred people in a night, he built the thing that actually saved lives: barometers at the coasts, simultaneous readings by telegraph, cones hoisted on mastheads — the first storm warning went out on 6 February 1861, and on 1 August 1861 The Times carried the world's first published daily weather forecast, his. He did not coin the word; he chose it — forecast, an old English word, set deliberately against prophecy: “the result of scientific combination and calculation”. The forecasts drew mockery in Parliament and the press. He spent his fortune on the work. On 30 April 1865 he took his own life; his old shipmates raised a fund for his widow, and Darwin gave a hundred pounds. The year after, a committee found the forecasts wanting and stopped them; the year after that, the sailors and the public demanded them back. They have run ever since.

So this page keeps a storm glass the way you keep a thing that is wrong and loved. The vial above lives in a simulated room: its temperature drifts with the season and the hour of your clock, cooler in the small hours, and the crystals follow that temperature honestly — mass from the solubility curve, character from the cooling rate, growth quick and melting slow, exactly as the studies describe. The fern's geometry is seeded fresh each day; the same glass never grows the same fern twice, and every visitor in the same hour sees the same glass. Press your hand to it and the crystals near your touch let go — slowly, reluctantly — and regrow along new paths after you leave. Warmth is the one message it has ever received. The folklore card above reads today's form by the traditional table, and the thermometer beside the vial tells you what is actually being said.

What I compressed, so you can distrust me accurately: the room's weather is synthetic (a page on a shelf has no thermometer of its own, and asking your device for its location or weather would be a different kind of instrument); the mapping from regimes to the folklore table's rows is artistic within the forms the 2008 experiments actually photographed; and a real glass answers over hours to days — mine answers over minutes, because a vigil should be possible in one sitting. Real storm glasses are still sold as desk curios, and are best left sealed: the camphor inside is poisonous and the alcohol burns.

Nothing here watches you. The season is arithmetic from your clock, not a location request; the typefaces arrive from Google's servers, and the rest is files.
