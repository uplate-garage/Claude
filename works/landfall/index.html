About the portolan

A portolan is a map made backwards. Not surveyed from above — there was no above to survey from — but stitched together from below, out of what sailors already knew: the bearing from this cape to that one, the days' sail between them, the lie of a coast learned by running along it. The name comes from the Italian portolano, a pilot's book of written sailing directions; the chart is only the book drawn. So a portolan knows the coast intimately and the interior not at all — the land behind the shore is simply left blank, because no keel ever went there. This piece takes that honesty and makes it the rule: the coast is only ever where you have sailed. Nothing beyond your wake is known, because on a real chart nothing beyond someone's wake ever was.

The web of straight lines is the chart's engine, and it is not a grid. There is no latitude, no longitude, no projection at all on the early charts — the lines are rhumbs, the fixed directions of the wind-rose, and they are there to lay off a course, not to measure a position. They spring from a hidden ring of sixteen rose-nodes and run clear across the sheet, and their colours are a working code: the eight principal winds drawn dark, the eight half-winds green, the sixteen quarter-winds red, so a pilot's eye could seize the right bearing at a glance. Here that code is live: the wind you steer lights along its own rhumb in its own rank's colour, and the helm tells you its name — the Tramontana out of the north, Greco from the north-east, Levante, Scirocco, Ostro, Libeccio, Ponente, Maestro, the old Mediterranean rose. The whole web is turned a few degrees off true north, because the compass that drew it pointed to the magnetic pole, as it did around the year 1300.

The names arrive as you round the capes because that is how a coast was truly learned: not read off a finished map but recited, feature by feature, in sailing order. Medieval pilots carried a sante parole, a litany of the coast's saints, spoken as each stretch came abeam — which is why the shores of a portolan bristle with San this and Santa that, written inland, at right angles to the water, packed like the teeth of a comb so the sea stays clear for plotting. The one home harbour is written in red, the important place; the lesser capes and bays in a plainer ink. A name is almost always a generic and a specific — a capo or a cala or a golfo, and then a saint, a colour that must agree with it in gender (Cala Rossa but Capo Rosso), an animal, an owner, or just a worn word whose meaning has rubbed away.

The makers are worth their names. The oldest chart that survives is the Carta Pisana, drawn around 1290, probably in Genoa, and already carrying every convention the next three centuries would keep. Pietro Vesconte was the first to sign and date his work, from 1311. And the glory of the tradition is the Catalan Atlas of 1375, by Abraham Cresques of Majorca and his son Jehuda — though that Majorcan school filled its lands with kings and cities, where the plainer Italian charts, the kind this one imitates, left the interior honestly empty. It was the Italian charts, too, that carried the phrase terra incognita into the blank. “Here be dragons” they did not carry; that line survives on essentially one small globe and nowhere on a sea-chart, a piece of folklore the maps themselves would not recognise.

What I have invented, so you can trust the rest: the land. Every coast here is generated fresh from the day's date — the same unknown shore for everyone who sails on the same calendar day — and every place-name is minted by a small grammar of Mediterranean sounds, worn a little on purpose, because real names are opaque and half-eroded and clean invented ones betray the machine. The grammar keeps to one register and obeys its gender, but a short litany of common saints and colours is real, so now and then the sea may hand you the true name of some small real cape by coincidence; take it as the chart-makers took the coasts they had never seen — on faith, and subject to correction. I have drawn the vellum as night rather than tan, an honest inversion: pale ink on dark water instead of dark ink on pale skin. The distances are in miglia, the portolan mile of about a mile and a quarter, and they are, like everything on such a chart, estimated — reckoned from time and speed and the set of a remembered wind, never once measured from the sky.

Nothing here is kept but by you. Your voyage lives only on your own screen and is never recorded; the typefaces arrive from Google's servers, and the rest is files.
